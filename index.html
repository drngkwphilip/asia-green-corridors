Anomaly watch — corridor hubs, self-graded

Two client-side detectors run on each hub's full multi-year daily series (bundled, harvested 2026-11-03): a structural detector (binary-segmented Pettitt changepoint, p<0.05, ≥25% level shift) and a transient detector (7-day mean below 60% of the trailing-90-day median for ≥3 days). Detections are matched against PortWatch's documented disruption events (±14 days). Assumptions (deliberately simple, stated for scrutiny): green-fuel share cuts emissions one-for-one on a well-to-wake basis; 10% slower steaming cuts fuel per voyage ≈19% (speed–power cube law) on the adopting share; shore power removes ≈4% of voyage emissions (auxiliary at berth) on covered calls; the modelled reduction phases in linearly 2026–2035 and then holds — r(t) = f(t)·[1 − (1 − g)K], with f(t) = (t − 2025)/10 clamped to [0, 1] (so f(2030) = 0.5). Reductions are applied to the measured 2025 baseline, whereas the IMO checkpoints are defined relative to 2008. We therefore overlay the IMO percentage bands on the 2025 baseline only as an approximation. If 2025 Asian shipping emissions exceed their 2008 level, the reductions required against the true IMO baseline would be greater than those shown here. Policy anchors: Hong Kong Action Plan targets 200,000 t green fuel/yr by 2030; Singapore targets >1 Mt low-carbon methanol supply by 2030 (Ng 2026).

What this is — and what it never claims

What it is. A free, keyless, browser-only observatory that brings together four public datasets normally viewed separately: satellite-AIS port activity (IMF PortWatch), satellite-estimated shipping emissions (Climate TRACE), documented disruption events (PortWatch), and 80 years of typhoon tracks (NOAA IBTrACS). It allows financiers, insurers and policy makers to screen any of 787 Asian ports in one click — a comparison that would otherwise require paid terminals or substantial manual data assembly.

What it never claims. (1) AIS activity cannot identify fuel type: tanker calls are used only as a proxy for liquid-bulk intensity. (2) Emissions are Climate TRACE satellite estimates, not measured fuel sales. (3) The scenario sandbox is a transparent heuristic, not a forecast; its assumptions are shown alongside the results. (4) A quiet week in port calls does not, by itself, prove disruption: changes should be judged against each port's own baseline, with "too few reports" treated as a valid result. (5) Nothing here constitutes investment, insurance or policy advice.

Policy context. The observatory was built as an empirical companion to Ng, K. W. P. (2026), From Port to Platform: Positioning Hong Kong as the Facilitator of the Middle East–Asia Green Marine Fuel Corridor, Zenodo (doi:10.5281/zenodo.21639017), whose limitations section notes that the corridor's quantitative evidence is "thin by construction". This tool provides an open, measured data layer; the institutional thesis it helps contextualise remains that of the working paper.

Self-grading anomaly detector — measured results (M1.5)

Why this panel exists. Tabs 1 and 2 draw conclusions from satellite ship-tracking data. Rather than asking users to accept those conclusions on trust, we test the dashboard's alarm system against a set of documented disruption events and publish the results — including where the method performs poorly. The validation uses two public IMF PortWatch datasets: daily port traffic from 2019–2026 and a documented disruption log from 2018–2026. It applies the same alarm logic used in Tab 2's "Anomaly watch" and is reproducible from validation/results.json in the repository. This panel evaluates the detection method as a whole; its results do not change when an individual port is selected.
How the test worksWhat we did — and where each number comes from
I · The exam questionsThe bundled file contains 132 disruption records; 130 have usable event dates and cover cyclones, floods, earthquakes and conflicts. Of these, 83 are usable for validation: the remainder are excluded because the satellite traffic record begins in January 2019 and the detector requires at least 60 days of "normal" traffic before an event to establish a baseline. We therefore do not test events for which the underlying data are insufficient.
II · The alarm being testedTwo detectors monitor each port's weekly-average traffic. A structural detector identifies persistent step-changes — a lasting increase or decrease of at least 25%. A transient detector identifies sudden dips, where traffic falls below a specified proportion of the port's own recent baseline for at least three days. Each row in the results table represents a different sensitivity setting.
III · Marking the examAn event is counted as caught if an alert is triggered at an affected port within ±14 days of the documented event dates. At the adopted setting, the detector caught 49 of 83 events (59.0%), as shown in the "Disruptions caught" column.
IV · Measuring alert frequency and matchesAcross the full observation period, the detector generated 3,109 alerts at the ports included in the validation exercise. Of these, 130 coincided with a documented event, equivalent to 4.2% of all alerts. Because the disruption log is not exhaustive — for example, COVID-19 congestion and the 2026 Gulf crisis are not catalogued as events — an unmatched alert is not necessarily a false alarm. The 4.2% figure is an observed event-match rate: temporal proximity does not by itself establish causation, and because the log is incomplete it cannot be read as an estimate of detection precision.

"Alerts per port per year" standardises total alert volume across all 283 monitored ports and the full observation period (1 January 2019 – 23 October 2026, 7.81 years). At the adopted setting, the detector generated 3,125 alerts across these ports: 3,125 ÷ 283 ÷ 7.81 ≈ 1.41 alerts per port per year. (The 3,109 figure above counts only alerts at event-affected ports, which are the alerts eligible for matching; the rate uses every monitored port, including quiet ones with no nearby documented events.)

What this means for you — in plain English:
The alarm detects more than half of documented disruptions at the adopted setting. It caught 49 of 83 documented events (59.0%). At the most sensitive setting tested, this rose to 63 of 83 (75.9%).
Greater sensitivity means more alerts. Across the settings tested, the catch rate rises from 33.7% to 75.9%, while alert frequency increases from 0.50 to 4.11 alerts per port per year. The appropriate balance depends on the user's purpose and tolerance for additional alerts; an insurer and a port operator may reasonably choose different settings.
The adopted setting follows a declared rule. We select the setting with the highest catch rate subject to no more than two alerts per port per year. More sensitive settings are reported alongside it rather than excluded.
The documented-event match rate is an observed minimum, not precision. An alert without a matching documented event is counted as unmatched, even though the event log is incomplete. Real disruptions that are absent from the log — including COVID-19 congestion and the 2026 Gulf crisis — therefore reduce the reported match rate, and a temporal match does not by itself prove the disruption caused the alert. For early-warning screening, the more informative comparison is between disruptions caught and alerts per port per year, rather than the match-rate column alone.
Detection varies by event type. At the adopted setting, the detector caught 38 of 62 cyclones and 6 of 8 floods, compared with only 3 of 10 earthquakes. This is consistent with what the detector measures: disruption to vessel activity rather than physical damage to port infrastructure.
The result is not driven by a single matching window. Changing the permitted detection window from 7 to 14 to 21 days moves the catch rate from 51.8% to 62.7%, indicating that the overall finding is not highly sensitive to this assumption.
Caveats. The event log is not exhaustive and a temporal match does not by itself establish causation, so the documented-event match rate is an observed minimum rather than a measure of true precision. The catch rates are in-sample: the operating-point rule is applied to the same events used for grading. Quiet ports are difficult to assess reliably, so the validation abstains below one call per day. AIS coverage also misses some small vessels.

For specialists. The structural detector uses binary-segmented Pettitt changepoints (p<0.05; minimum segment 90 days; ≥25% level shift; ≤8 splits). The transient detector uses a seven-day trailing mean below the specified fraction of the trailing 90-day median for at least three days, closing at ≥80% recovery. The matching window is ±14 days around documented event dates. The operating point is selected using a declared rule: the highest catch rate subject to ≤2 alerts per port-year. At the adopted setting, results by event type are: cyclones 38/62; floods 6/8; earthquakes 3/10; other 2/2; volcano 0/1. The Asian subset records 30/54 events caught (55.6%). Full event-level results are available in validation/results.json.

Intended use & disclosure

This observatory is designed for decision-support screening by project financiers, investors, insurers and policy makers. It is not a trading system, a compliance calculation, or a substitute for verified IMO DCS or EU MRV data. The tool was developed with AI assistance (Kimi AI); all data harvesting, modelling choices and text were reviewed by the author. It uses a zero-backend architecture: no account is required, no user activity is tracked, and no data leave the browser other than direct requests to the public sources identified above.
